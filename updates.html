The Build Log

Engineering Updates

Wavie has been built in the open for over a year and a half — from the first rough CAD sketch to real hardware on the bench. This is the whole thread, month by month, exactly as it happened.

July 2026 Hardware Mechanical Latest

Full CAD Freeze: The Whole System Comes Together

July 29, 2026  •  5 min read

After eighteen months, the geometry is frozen. The complete Wavie assembly is modeled and fit-checked end to end — three tiers, optics up top, fluidics in the middle, electronics at the base, all packed into a single sealed tower barely taller than a banana.

Everything below in this log led here: the reagent carousel that became a rotary valve, the peristaltic pump we designed part by part, the spectrophotometer optical cell, and the ESP32-S3 mainboard with its isolated probe inputs. With CAD locked, we're heading into the first full beta build.

April 2026

Real hardware, real water

After months on a screen, this was the month Wavie became physical. The fabricated mainboard came back from the fab house, the first optical bench tests pushed real light through real cuvettes, the probe kit arrived for calibration, and we printed and test-fit the rotary valve rotor. Peak messy-bench season — and the most fun we've had on the project.

April 24, 2026

The rotary valve rotor, in hand

The eight-port selector rotor came off the printer. Six reagents, a sample line, and waste — eight ports, 45° apart, one rotor. We spent the evening test-fitting it against the glass cell and the barbed tube fittings. It indexes cleanly.

April 21, 2026

First light through a cuvette

Bench-testing the optical path for the first time: a real glass spectrophotometer cuvette beside our printed holder, dialing in the 10.6 mm path length. This little cell is the heart of every colorimetric test — get the geometry and light-tightness right and the rest is math.

April 21, 2026

Probes arrive for calibration

The pH and ORP probes showed up with their buffer sachets (6.86, 9.18, 10.01). Wavie's probe channels are galvanically isolated, so each one gets calibrated against known references before we trust a single reading off the tank.

April 2, 2026

The chassis stands up

The printed three-tier chassis, the pump, the tubing, and the wiring all mounted together for the first time. It's rough and half the cables are jumpers — but it stands, and the fluid path runs continuously from sample line to cell to waste.

March 2026

From screen to bench

March was the jump from CAD to atoms. We printed the first full three-tier chassis, the V0 mainboard came back populated, the addressable status strip lit up on first boot, and we kicked off the sibling Wavie Power board — smart switched outlets on the same bus Wavie already speaks.

March 30, 2026

The board is real

Holding the populated ESP32-S3 mainboard for the first time — USB-C, 24 V barrel jack, isolated BNC inputs for the pH/ORP probes, and JST connectors for everything else. Bring-up starts now.

March 30, 2026

Status strip, lit

The 33-LED addressable status strip running its first rainbow straight off the board. A small thing — but seeing first-boot firmware drive real light is the moment a PCB stops being a render and starts being a computer.

March 28, 2026

Wavie Power takes shape

Started the sibling board: eight switched AC outlets, a relay array, and its own microcontroller riding the same CAN bus Wavie already carries. Pumps, lights, and heaters on one app, one ecosystem — the plan we hinted at in the product line, now in layout.

March 5, 2026

First full print

The first complete three-tier chassis off the resin printer, loosely populated with the pump, the valve, and a stepper to check fit and tubing routing. Held together with hope and a few zip ties, but it's the first time the whole thing existed off-screen.

January 2026

The app, and one full assembly

Two big firsts. The mobile app got its first real screens — a live dashboard, reagent tracking, and dosing control — and every subsystem finally came together in one clean assembly. The pieces stopped being separate projects and started being a product.

January 21, 2026

One clean assembly

Every subsystem in a single model: optics and its stepper up top, the rotary valve and home switch in the middle, the pump stack and mainboard at the base. First time it all fit in one tower without something overlapping something else.

January 12, 2026

Meet the app

The first real UI. A dashboard with live probe tiles and colorimetric history for all five wet-chemistry parameters — alkalinity, calcium, magnesium, nitrate, phosphate — plus reagent levels and dosing control. The Dose and Power tabs are already stubbed in: this is where Wavie, Wavie Dose, and Wavie Power become one system.

November 2025

The electronics come alive

The month Wavie stopped being a mechanism and became a computer. The optical measurement head matured, the full tower assembly came together, and the V0 AutoTester mainboard went from schematic to autorouted to rendered — ESP32-S3, dual stepper drivers, isolated probe front-ends, and (naturally) a pufferfish hidden in the silkscreen.

November 19, 2025

Board, meet body

Dropped the freshly-designed mainboard into the full tower assembly. For the first time, optics, fluidics, and brains all shared one model — and the BNC probe jacks lined up with the enclosure exactly where they needed to.

November 18, 2025

The V0 mainboard, routed

The first full mainboard: an ESP32-S3, two TMC stepper drivers for the pump and valve, isolated pH/ORP front-ends on BNC, and a CAN bus for future modules. Autorouted overnight, cleaned up by hand, and stamped with a little pufferfish because a board should have some personality.

November 12, 2025

The measurement head, exploded

The optics module laid open: three wavelengths of LED, a light detector, and the beam path running straight down through the cuvette. This is how Wavie reads chemistry — measuring how much light a developed sample absorbs, no test strips or color cards anywhere.

October 2025

Pump and optics, from scratch

We stopped bolting together off-the-shelf subassemblies and started designing our own. The peristaltic pump got built up part by part, and the optical cell got its first real cross-sections — the two subsystems accuracy depends on most.

October 28, 2025

Sectioning the measurement cell

The first real cross-section through the optical cell, with the LED, detector, and cuvette on a single axis. Getting this geometry right — and the chamber light-tight — is the whole ballgame for colorimetric accuracy, so it got a lot of attention this month.

October 28, 2025

One pump, designed part by part

We designed our own peristaltic pump head from the ground up — rotor, rollers, and the C-shaped occlusion track that squeezes the tube. The goal: one bidirectional pump that can meter every fluid in the system to a couple hundredths of a milliliter, with nothing but silicone tube ever touching the sample.

August 2025

The valve, the shell, the mark

A huge mechanical month. The rotary selector valve got engineered properly, the interior was integrated for the first time, and the enclosure got its industrial-design pass — including the crescent that's now our mark.

August 25, 2025

The shell

The first proper enclosure: a tall rounded tower with the crescent embossed near the base and scalloped slots for tubing along the bottom edge. The whole stack lifts out as one — no fighting a tangle of parts to service it.

August 19, 2025

Eight ports, one valve

The rotary distribution valve took its real shape: six reagents, a sample line, and waste across eight ports exactly 45° apart, driven straight off a stepper with a single home switch. One rigid rotary index instead of a manifold full of solenoids and tubing.

August 14, 2025

Everything, inside

First time the pump, the valve, and the cuvette holder all lived inside one case in CAD. Cramped, and a couple of parts were fighting for the same 3 mm — but it fit, and the "compact-first" bet started to look real.

June–July 2025

Going vertical

Two decisions this summer set the shape of the entire project: the brains and the body. We committed to an ESP32-S3 for the controller and pivoted the whole form factor from a wide box to a tall, compact tower.

July 22, 2025

The tower takes shape

Committed to a vertical tower: a slim faceplate with the reagent-well windows stacked down the front. From here on, every design decision had to earn its footprint. Compact-first.

June 24, 2025

Picking the brains

Settled on the ESP32-S3 — Wi-Fi, plenty of PSRAM, and enough I/O to drive two steppers, the optics bus, an addressable LED strip, and five probe channels at once. An Arduino simply couldn't carry all of it.

March–April 2025

Where it started

The very beginning — rough parts and one big idea: a carousel of reagents feeding an automated measurement cell, so a reef tank could test itself without anyone pipetting anything.

April 13, 2025

The reagent carousel

The first real architecture idea: an eight-well disc with a central manifold, so one mechanism could select from every reagent in turn. The carousel eventually evolved into today's rotary valve — but the core bet, "one moving part to reach every fluid," started right here.

March 29, 2025

First parts

The first crude CAD — flat plates, pockets, a hollow block. Nothing you'd photograph for a brochure, but every project starts with a few ugly parts and a stubborn idea. This one just happened to keep going for eighteen months.
