Spring 2026

Real Hardware on the Bench

The device left the screen. The first full chassis came off the resin printer, the custom mainboards came back from the fab and powered up on the first try, and the first real optical and probe tests began. One honest snag turned into a design change: the addressable light strip wouldn't run cleanly off the ESP32's 3.3 V data line — it needed a logic level shifter, which is exactly why the lighting became its own dedicated board.

Early 2026

Software Meets Hardware

The app got its first real screens — a dashboard with live probe tiles and colorimetric history for all five wet-chemistry parameters, plus reagent levels and dosing, with the Dose and Power tabs already stubbed in. Around the same time, every subsystem finally came together in one clean model.

Late 2025

The Custom Electronics & Optics

Wavie became a computer. We designed our own ESP32-S3 mainboard — the V0 "AutoTester," pufferfish "Le Fish" and all — reading pH and ORP. Then we redesigned it from the ground up: every analog channel got fully isolated, and the probe suite grew from two channels to five, adding dissolved oxygen, salinity, and temperature. A sibling Wavie Power board of switched outlets rides the same CAN bus. And the heart of the chemistry read — a spectrophotometer that measures light at three wavelengths through the sample — took shape alongside it.

2025 · the big pivot

One Valve to Replace Eight

This was the central mechanical problem of the whole project. Eight individual pinch valves — the approach from the very first bench — is a lot of things that can fail. So we consolidated them into a single motor-driven rotary valve: one disc, eight ports (six reagents, sample, and waste), 45° apart, paired with one custom peristaltic pump that meters every fluid. The valve was designed in CAD, printed, and proven on a scrappy blue test frame — the frame the whole current design was later built around.

2025

A Compact Tower — and a First Booth

The form factor came together: a tall, compact tower where every part has to earn its footprint, wrapped in an enclosure carrying the crescent-ripple mark you now see on the pump and the boards. We were confident enough to take an early prototype — Wavie and Wavie Power — to a trade-show booth. It was very early days: the booth pulled barely a glance, and honestly the project has changed almost beyond recognition since. But standing there with a real box on the table lit a fire under the whole build.

Early 2025

First Lines in CAD

With the concept proven, the design work — and the learning curve — began. The earliest parts were built to hold the pinch valves, and the architecture kept reshaping itself: a body to ring the valves around a central manifold, then a reagent carousel meant to let one mechanism reach every fluid. The carousel didn't survive, but that idea — one mechanism, every fluid — became the rotary valve.

Where it began

The Proof of Concept

Before any of it, Wavie was a plywood board on a desk. The premise had to be proven first: could you meter reef reagents, mix a sample in a small cell, and read the result automatically, with cheap parts? So we built exactly that — eight hobby servos, each squeezing a silicone tube, feeding a passive spider manifold, driven by an Arduino, with real ABC Alkalinity and Ca/Mg reagents in the loop. It was ugly, and it worked. Everything since has been about making it small, tight, and reliable — starting with one hard truth: eight pinch valves is a lot of things that can fail.
