The Build Log

The Whole Story

Wavie has been built in the open for over a year and a half — from a plank of plywood and eight pinch valves to real hardware on the bench, a trade-show booth, and a first full prototype now in sight. Newest updates first — scroll down to go all the way back to where it started.

July 2026 Hardware Where we are now

CAD Freeze — and the Beta Waitlist Opens

July 29, 2026

Eighteen months after that plank of plywood, the geometry is frozen. The complete Wavie assembly is modeled and fit-checked end to end — three tiers, optics up top, fluidics in the middle, electronics at the base, all in a single sealed tower barely taller than a banana.

Every step below led here: the pinch-valve proof-of-concept that became a single rotary valve, the pump we designed part by part, the spectrophotometer cell, and the ESP32-S3 mainboard with its isolated probe inputs. The addressable lighting even earned its own board — more on that just below. We're almost ready to show the first full prototype, so the beta waitlist is now open: a $500 deposit holds your place at the front of the line for the first units, and is credited in full toward your Wavie when the price is set.

April 2026

Real hardware, real water

The month Wavie became fully physical. The fabricated mainboard powered up, the first optical bench tests pushed real light through real cuvettes, the probe kit arrived for calibration, and we printed and test-fit the rotary valve rotor. Peak messy-bench season.

April 24, 2026

The rotary valve rotor, in hand

The eight-port selector rotor came off the printer — the direct descendant of that eight-pinch-valve mess from the very first bench. Six reagents, sample, waste; one rotor; 45° between ports. Test-fitting against the glass cell, it indexes cleanly.

April 21, 2026

First light through a cuvette

Bench-testing the optical path for the first time: a real glass cuvette beside our printed holder, dialing in the 10.6 mm path length. This little cell is the heart of every colorimetric test.

April 7, 2026

Probes arrive for calibration

The pH and ORP probes showed up (hand-labeled "PH-probe" and "ORP-probe") with their buffer sachets — 6.86, 9.18, 10.01. Wavie's probe channels are galvanically isolated, so each one gets calibrated against known references before we trust a reading off the tank.

April 2, 2026

The chassis stands up

The printed three-tier chassis, the pump, the tubing, and the wiring all mounted together on the bench. Rough, half of it jumper wires — but it stands, and the fluid path runs continuously from sample line to cell to waste.

March 2026

From screen to bench

The jump from CAD to atoms. The V0.1 boards came back from the fab, powered up first try, and drove their first LEDs — where we hit a real bug. We also refined the enclosure in Blender and started the sibling Wavie Power board.

March 31, 2026

Boards from the fab

A panel of five Wavie V0.1 mainboards, fresh off a lead-free blue-soldermask run and across an ocean by FedEx. Nothing beats the box-opening on a board you've only ever seen on a screen.

March 30, 2026

Power-on, first try

USB-C in, and the power LED came up green — no smoke, no reworks. The ESP32-S3, the regulators, and the probe front-ends all came alive on the first attempt. A very good day.

March 30, 2026

First light on the strip — and a gotcha

The addressable RGB strip lit up in full rainbow… but not reliably. The ESP32's 3.3 V data line couldn't drive the strip's 5 V input cleanly — a classic case for a logic level shifter. That finding is exactly what led to a dedicated lighting board (see the top of this log).

March 28, 2026

Wavie Power takes shape

Started the sibling board: eight switched AC outlets, a relay array, and its own microcontroller riding the same CAN bus Wavie already carries. Pumps, lights, and heaters on one app.

March 22, 2026

Detailing the enclosure

Refining the full cutaway in CAD and taking the outer shell into Blender to work the surface finish and texture — the step that turns a functional box into something you'd want on your stand.

March 5, 2026

First full print

The first complete three-tier chassis off the resin printer, loosely populated with the pump, valve, and a stepper to check fit and tubing routing. Held together with hope and a few zip ties.

February 2026

Off the screen

The first assembled chassis on the bench — pump, stepper, and tubing routed through the three tiers. Still driven by breadboard electronics, but the mechanical stack was finally something you could hold.

February 4, 2026

The stack takes physical form

Optics and pump up top, the valve deck in the middle, a NEMA stepper at the base — with the tell-tale four-colour stepper wiring and silicone tubing threaded through. The proportions from CAD held up in real plastic.

January 2026

The app, and one full assembly

Two big firsts. The mobile app got its first real screens, and every subsystem finally came together in one clean model.

January 21, 2026

One clean assembly

Every subsystem in a single model: optics and its stepper up top, the rotary valve and home switch in the middle, the pump and mainboard at the base. First time it all fit in one tower without something overlapping something else.

January 12, 2026

Meet the app

The first real UI. A dashboard with live probe tiles and colorimetric history for all five wet-chemistry parameters — alkalinity, calcium, magnesium, nitrate, phosphate — plus reagent levels and dosing control. The Dose and Power tabs are already stubbed in: this is where Wavie, Wavie Dose, and Wavie Power become one system.

November 2025

The electronics come alive

The month Wavie became a computer. The optics module matured, the cuvette cell got printed and held, and the V0 AutoTester mainboard went from schematic to autorouted to rendered — ESP32-S3, two stepper drivers, isolated probe front-ends, and a pufferfish named "Le Fish" in the silkscreen.

November 19, 2025

Board, meet body

Dropped the freshly-designed mainboard into the full tower assembly. For the first time, optics, fluidics, and brains all shared one model.

November 18, 2025

Routing the V0 board — "Le Fish"

The first full mainboard, routed: ESP32-S3, dual TMC stepper drivers, isolated pH/ORP front-ends, and a CAN bus for future modules. Signed and dated in the copper — V0 AutoTester Dev Board, Evan Croft, 11/17/25 — with a pufferfish in the corner, because a board should have some personality. The 3D render followed the same night.

November 7, 2025

Holding the measurement cell

The clear printed cuvette cell in hand, in front of its CAD model — the little square chamber where sample and reagent mix and the light gets read. Printing it clear enough to pass a clean beam is its own small battle.

November 4, 2025

The measurement head, exploded

The optics module laid open: three wavelengths of LED, a light detector, and the beam path running straight down through the cuvette. This is how Wavie reads chemistry — measuring how much light a developed sample absorbs, no test strips anywhere.

October 2025

The rotary valve becomes real

The rotary valve left the screen. We printed the eight-tube manifold and the valve rotor, and built the first working rotary valve on a translucent blue test frame — the frame the entire current redesign was later based on. The pump got built up part by part, too.

October 28, 2025

The blue-frame rotary valve

A NEMA stepper on a translucent blue printed cage, driving the first real rotary valve with its silicone tubes fanning out below. This scrappy test rig matters more than it looks: its frame became the starting point the whole new design was built around.

October 27, 2025

Manifold and rotor, printed

The clear eight-tube manifold and the valve rotor in hand, with the stainless check balls that seat inside. Eight tubes in, one rotating selector — the mechanism that replaced eight separate pinch valves.

October 28, 2025

A pump built part by part

We designed our own peristaltic pump head from the ground up — rotor, rollers, and the C-shaped occlusion track that squeezes the tube — and sectioned the optical cell to line up the LED, detector, and cuvette on one axis.

August 2025

One valve to replace eight — and a booth

A huge month. The eight-pinch-valve fluidics was finally designed out: a single motor-driven rotary pinch valve (one disc, eight ports, 45° apart) took its place. The pump got its badge, the interior was integrated, the enclosure got its industrial-design pass — and we showed the whole idea off at a trade show.

August 30, 2025

Wavie, at a trade show

We took Wavie and Wavie Power to a booth — right next to Coral Essentials. The banner said it plainly: hands-off reef stability, compact and serviceable, testing calcium, alkalinity, magnesium, nitrate and phosphate, tracking temperature, pH and ORP, with an 8-port rotary pinch valve and programmable outlets. Real people, real reactions — a long way from a plank of plywood.

August 25, 2025

The shell

The first proper enclosure: a tall rounded tower with the crescent-ripple mark embossed near the base and scalloped slots for tubing. The whole stack lifts out as one.

August 19, 2025

Designing the rotary pinch valve

Modeling the rotary valve body and its circular eight-tube manifold in CAD — the part that lets one motor and one disc do what eight servo-driven pinch valves used to. The pump module also picked up its blue concentric-ripple badge this month, the mark you now see everywhere.

August 14, 2025

Everything, inside — for the first time

The pump, the selector, and the cell all packed into one case in CAD. Cramped, with parts fighting for the same three millimetres — but it fit, and "compact-first" stopped being a slogan.

June–July 2025

Going vertical

Two decisions set the shape of everything that followed: the brains and the body. We committed to an ESP32-S3, and pivoted the whole form factor from a wide box to a tall, compact tower.

July 22, 2025

The tower takes shape

Committed to a vertical tower: a slim faceplate with the reagent-well windows stacked down the front. From here on, every part had to earn its footprint.

June 24, 2025

Picking the brains

Settled on the ESP32-S3 — Wi-Fi, plenty of PSRAM, and enough I/O to drive two steppers, the optics bus, an addressable LED strip, and five probe channels at once. The Arduino from the proof-of-concept couldn't carry all of it.

April 2025

The reagent carousel

The first attempt to tame the pinch-valve sprawl in CAD: put every reagent well on one rotating disc around a central manifold. The carousel didn't survive to the final design — but "one mechanism reaches every fluid" did, and it's the DNA of today's rotary valve.

April 13, 2025

An eight-well disc

Eight reagent wells arranged around a disc, with a central multi-port hub at the middle — rotate to a well, draw from it, move on.

April 9, 2025

Nozzles and funnels

Small experiments on how fluid actually enters and leaves the cell — geometry that would take another year to really get right.

March 2025

First lines in CAD

With the concept proven on the bench, the real design work — and the learning curve — began. The very first parts were built to hold the pinch valves; from there, the whole architecture kept reshaping itself.

March 29, 2025

A disc to hold the pinch valves

This hollow disc was meant to hold the pinch valves in a ring around the central manifold. It didn't stay a disc for long: it became a square to pack the valves, then grew to hold the cuvette and pump, then got a whole new manifold once the rotary pinch valve replaced the individual ones. The design changed completely, over and over, in these early months.

March 29, 2025

Part number one

The very first modeled part — a flat plate with a raised boss and a square pocket. Nothing you'd photograph for a brochure, but it's the seed the whole enclosure grew from.

Prologue Fluidics Optics

Where It Started: A Plank and a Pile of Pinch Valves

Before a single polished render, Wavie was a plywood board on a desk. The whole premise had to be proven first: could you meter reef reagents, mix a sample in a small cell, and read the result — automatically, with cheap parts? So we built exactly that. Eight hobby servos, each squeezing a silicone tube — pinch valves, all of them — fed a passive 3D-printed spider manifold. An Arduino drove it, and real ABC Alkalinity and Ca/Mg reagents went in the loop. It was ugly and it worked, and it made the whole project real.

The proof-of-concept rig: eight servo-driven pinch valves feeding a central spider manifold, with the branded pump up top.

Running a real test — ABC Alkalinity and Ca/Mg reagents, cups of developed blue sample.

The very first pinch valve. This exact form was scrapped, but the pinch-valve principle — a motor squeezing a tube — carried all the way to the final rotary valve.

Everything above is the story of taking this pile of parts and making it small, tight, and reliable — starting with a hard truth from this rig: eight servos and eight pinch valves is a lot of things that can fail. Consolidating that mess into a single rotary valve became the central mechanical problem of the next year.
